# Reporting of Suspicious Transactions

## Connections

### [[AMLCTF Compliance Function|AML/CTF Compliance Function]] — `references` [EXTRACTED]
- **What this link tells you:** When assigning accountability for SAR filing, locate it within the AML/CTF compliance function rather than diffusely across the firm. Section 9.3 makes the compliance function officer responsible for 'receiving reports on suspicious ML/TF transactions' and 'reporting suspicious transactions to the SAFIU as soon as the suspicion is detected... as stated in the Reporting of Suspicious Transactions Section,' expressly cross-referring to Section 8. Combined with the independence requirement (officer reports to the board, has timely access to all data), you conclude the designated compliance officer is the accountable owner of the reporting duty in Section 8, and that board-approved procedures identifying the responsible person (8.1) must align with this function.
- **Grounding — this node (Page 52 / Section 8.1(e)):** "Determining the employee or officer responsible for reporting to the SAFIU about suspicious transactions."
- **Grounding — related node (Page 57 / Section 9.3(e)):** "Reporting suspicious transactions to the SAFIU as soon as the suspicion is detected... as stated in the Reporting of Suspicious Transactions Section."

### [[Monitoring of Transactions and Activities]] — `conceptually_related_to` [EXTRACTED]
- **What this link tells you:** Treat transaction monitoring and suspicious-transaction reporting as one continuous obligation chain, not two isolated duties: monitoring is the trigger mechanism whose output feeds the SAR duty. Section 7, grounded in Article (13) AML Law / Article (69) CTF Law, requires ongoing monitoring precisely so the institution can 'identify and report any suspicious transactions or activities to the SAFIU,' and Section 8 then imposes the immediate-reporting duty (8.3) covering even unsuccessful attempts (8.4). You would conclude that a monitoring deficiency directly undermines SAR compliance, and that once suspicion arises the reporting obligation is mandatory and time-critical — reasonable grounds alone trigger it regardless of transaction completion.
- **Grounding — this node (Page 52 / Section 8.3):** "The financial institution shall immediately and directly inform the SAFIU upon suspicion or the presence of information or reasonable grounds to suspect that a customer's behavior is related to ML/TF acts."
- **Grounding — related node (Page 48 / Section 7):** "monitoring of transactions and activities... enables the financial institution to identify and report any suspicious transactions or activities to the SAFIU."

### [[Saudi Arabia Financial Intelligence Unit (SAFIU)]] — `references` [EXTRACTED]
- **What this link tells you:** When assessing a financial institution's AML/CTF reporting obligations, treat the SAFIU as the mandatory recipient that gives the suspicious-transaction regime its legal effect: the duty to report is discharged only by filing to SAFIU in its approved form, immediately upon suspicion, including unsuccessful attempts. The link tells you the reporting rules in Section 8 are not discretionary internal escalation — they culminate in an external statutory disclosure to a named authority, backed by Article 13 of the AML Law and Article 69 of the CTF Law. Conclude that any compliance review must verify not just internal detection but timely, correctly-formatted SAFIU submission plus the required six-month technical report; do not treat closed internal cases as satisfying the duty where reasonable grounds existed.
- **Grounding — this node (Page 52 / para 8.3):** "shall immediately and directly inform the SAFIU upon suspicion or the presence of information or reasonable grounds to suspect that a customer's behavior is related to ML/TF acts"
- **Grounding — related node (Page 53 / para 8.6):** "The financial institution shall not submit any report to the SAFIU based on speculation or lack of information or reasonable grounds for suspicion."
